How to use this

Read the reference tabs once. Then live in the Simulators, Drills and Scenarios — that is where it sticks.

The idea underneath all of rigging: every load hangs on a load path — structure, attachment, sling, shackle, motor, truss, fixture — and the whole path is only as strong as its weakest piece. Your job is to know what every piece is rated for, keep every piece inside that rating (angles included), and tag out anything you would not hang over your own head.

Hardware

Every piece of rigging hardware carries a rating. Learn to find it, read it, and know what changes it.

WLL, breaking strength, design factor

Minimum breaking strength (MBS)

The load at which the part actually fails. You never rig to this number.

Working load limit (WLL)

The most you are allowed to put on it in service. WLL = MBS ÷ design factor. It is stamped, tagged or printed on the part.

Design factor (DF)

The margin the manufacturer built in. 5:1 is the common minimum for rigging hardware and slings; performer flying uses more (often 10:1). The factor is already inside the WLL — don't apply it twice.

What does reduce a WLL: the hitch (choker, basket), the angle (sling angle, side-loaded shackle or eye bolt), shock loading, and damage. Those are real reductions you must account for. Re-dividing a WLL by the design factor is not — that is double-counting.

Shackles

Anchor (bow) shackle

Round bow. The standard for arena work: takes two bridle legs in the bow. Screw-pin is what you will handle most.

Chain (D) shackle

Straight sides. For a single in-line pull only — not for multiple legs.

  1. Read the bow. Size and WLL are forged into it. No markings, no use.
  2. Pin fully seated. Screw it in hand-tight, fully engaged, shoulder against the bow. A pin that is not fully threaded is not rated.
  3. Mouse it (seize with wire or a zip-tie as specified) when it will hang for the show or could be rotated by the load.
  4. Multiple legs go in the bow, the single piece goes on the pin. Keep the bow's included angle at or under 120°.
  5. Load in line. Side loading cuts capacity — a common manufacturer table: 45° off line → about 70% of WLL; 90° → about 50%.
  6. Never swap the pin for a bolt, and never mix pins between shackles.

Steel (wire rope slings)

“Steel” in an arena means wire-rope slings with eyes on both ends, cut to standard lengths (2′, 5′, 10′, 20′, 30′, 50′) and added together to make basket and bridle legs. 6×19 and 6×37 with an independent wire rope core (IWRC) are what you will see.

6×19 vs 6×37

Six strands of 19 or 37 wires. More, thinner wires = more flexible, less resistant to crushing and abrasion.

Protect it at the beam

Steel wrapped around a beam gets a softener — burlap, a sleeve or a pad — at every edge. Sharp flange edges cut wire and kill the bend.

Kink, birdcage, crush

Any of these permanently damages the rope. It does not get straightened and reused. Tag it out.

Round slings (spansets)

Polyester core yarns inside a protective cover. Light, kind to finishes, and the cover colour tells you the rating. The tag is the rating — no tag, no lift.

Industry color code for polyester round slings (WSTDA). A choker hitch is roughly 80% of vertical. A basket only doubles the rating when both legs hang vertical — spread them and the angle factor takes it back. Always confirm on the tag.

Chain

Grade 80 / 100 alloy

The only chain for overhead lifting. Look for the grade stamp (8, 80, 10, 100) on the links.

Grade 30 / 43 / 70

Proof coil, high-test, transport chain. Not for overhead lifting, no matter how thick it looks.

Deck chain / trim chain

Short rated chain used to fine-tune a basket or bridle leg to the exact length. Shackle into a link, never hook a link sideways.

Points & Motors

An arena has no grid you can use as-is. Every show, the rig is built from points hung on the building's steel.

Who does what

Up rigger

Works in the steel. Sets the basket or beam wrap at the plotted location, drops a rope, hauls up the hardware, builds the point. Tied off 100% of the time.

Down rigger (ground rigger)

Works on the floor. Finds the mark, builds the hardware bundle or bridle to the plot, ties it on, sends it up, hangs the motor. Keeps the floor under the work clear.

Head rigger

Owns the plot, the loads and the call. Checks every point before it takes weight. Your questions go here.

From chalk mark to loaded hook

  1. Mark the floor. Points are laid out from the rigging plot off a reference (usually centre line and a datum like the downstage edge). Each mark carries the point number and its type — dead hang or bridle, leg lengths, load.
  2. Up rigger finds the steel over the mark and calls “Rope!” before dropping a hand line to it.
  3. Down rigger builds the bundle to the plot: steel, shackles, deck chain, softeners. For a bridle, both legs are built on the floor so the apex hangs at the plotted spot.
  4. Haul it up — on the hand line or a motor, never over anyone. Everyone else stays out from under it.
  5. Up rigger sets the basket around the beam with softeners, shackles it, mouses what needs mousing.
  6. Motor goes on. Hook into the point (or the point into the motor, for a body-up hang), latch closed, chain checked for twist.
  7. Head rigger checks the point before it takes weight.

Chain motors

The usual motor

A 1-ton or ½-ton electric chain hoist (the CM Lodestar is the classic). Rated WLL is on the body. Chain runs into a chain bag that must hold all the chain.

Climbing vs body-up

Climbing (common on tour): motor is on the truss, hook goes to the point, motor climbs the chain. Body-up: motor hangs from the point and the hook lifts the load.

Limit switches

Upper and lower limits stop the hook before it hits the body (two-blocking) or runs out of chain. Never bypass them.

Running backwards

Hit UP and it goes DOWN? Stop. The motor is out of phase — and on a reversed motor the limit switches will not protect you. Fix it at the controller/cable, not by holding the other button.

D8 vs D8+

Hoist classes from the European standard most productions follow. D8+ hoists have redundant brakes and a higher design factor so they can hold a load over people; a plain D8 hoist over people needs a secondary safety.

Load cells

On bigger rigs a load cell at each point reads actual weight live. A point reading high usually means something is snagged or the truss is out of level.

Before a point leaves the floor

Bridle Math

A bridle puts a point where no beam is. Two legs from two beams meet at an apex. The legs always carry more than their share of the load — how much more depends on the angle.

h = horizontal distance from the apex to each beam · v = vertical drop from the beams to the apex · L = leg length. The apex sits over the load, not halfway between the beams, unless the bridle is symmetrical.

The three formulas

You wantFormulaPlain words
Leg lengthL = √(h² + v²)Pythagoras. Round up to the steel and chain you have.
Each leg's share of the weightV1 = W × h2 ÷ (h1 + h2)The leg closer to the load carries more. Symmetrical: each carries W ÷ 2.
Leg tensionT = V × L ÷ vShare of the weight times leg length over drop. Always bigger than V.
Worked example. 1,000 lb load, beams 6′ either side of the apex, 8′ drop. L = √(36 + 64) = 10′. Each leg's share = 500 lb. Tension = 500 × 10 ÷ 8 = 625 lb per leg. Both legs together carry 1,250 lb of tension to hold up 1,000 lb — that extra is what the angle costs you.

The angle table

Leg angle from horizontalIncluded angle at apexEach leg (symmetrical)Verdict
90° (vertical)0°50% of WDead hang
60°60°58% of WComfortable
45°90°71% of WCommon, fine
30°120°100% of WThe limit
15°150°193% of WNever

Keep the included angle at or under 120°. At 120° each leg carries the whole load. Past it, each leg carries more than the load itself, and the sideways pull on the beams climbs fast. Sling angle factor = 1 ÷ sin(angle from horizontal).

Unequal bridles

Different h on each side. The short-side leg is steeper and carries more of the weight; check both legs — the long one is flatter, so its tension can still be high.

Beams at different heights

Then v is different for each leg and the simple formulas above don't hold. That's the head rigger's or the software's calculation.

Horizontal pull

Every bridle leg pulls the beam sideways as well as down. Buildings are designed for down. That's why bridle points are on the venue's approved list, not wherever they fit.

Fly Systems

Theatres have a permanent system. Counterweight is most common; hemp is still around. Most theatres also have a fire curtain you must never block.

Counterweight line set

Batten (pipe)→ Lift lines→ Loft blocks→ Head block→ Arbor (counterweight)

Arbor

Carriage that holds the counterweight bricks, kept in place by spreader plates and a locking plate on top. Rides in a T-track or wire guides.

Purchase (operating) line

The rope the flyman pulls. Runs from the top of the arbor over the head block, down through the rope lock on the lock rail, around the tension block at the floor, back to the bottom of the arbor.

Rope lock

Holds a balanced set in place. It is not a brake and is not designed to hold a set that is badly out of balance.

Short, centre, long lines

Lift lines are named by their distance from the head block: short line is nearest the fly gallery side, long line is farthest.

Loading and unloading — the rule

Loading: load on the batten first, then counterweight in the arbor. Unloading: counterweight out of the arbor first, then load off the batten.
During loading the batten is at its low position and the arbor is up at the loading gallery. If the arbor ever outweighs the batten, the arbor falls and the batten flies out — a runaway. Keep the batten side heavy until the set is balanced.
  1. Talk first. Loading gallery and deck agree on what is coming on or off, and how much.
  2. Rope lock on, set at its low trim. Nobody unlocks a set that is being loaded.
  3. Batten load goes on — scenery, lights, pipe weight.
  4. Counterweight goes in at the loading gallery, spreader plates as you go, locking plate on top. Call “Heads!” and never lean over the rail.
  5. Check balance with a hand on the purchase line before the lock comes fully off. It should move with steady effort and stay where you leave it.

Words you'll hear

Trim

The set's height for the show (in-trim / out-trim). Marked on the purchase line with spike tape.

Arbor heavy / pipe heavy

Which side outweighs the other. A slightly pipe-heavy set is safer than an arbor-heavy one.

Hemp set

Rope lines tied off (belayed) to pins on a pin rail, balanced by sandbags or muscle. No mechanical counterweight — the operator is the brake.

Fire curtain

Drops by gravity to separate stage from house. Nothing — cable, scenery, a ladder, a road case — ever sits in its path.

Mechanical advantage

RigSupporting partsTheoretical MA
Single whip11:1 (direction change only)
Gun tackle22:1
Luff tackle33:1
Double purchase44:1

Count the rope parts supporting the moving block. Friction costs roughly 10% per sheave, so the real advantage is always less than the count. Don't confuse the tackle above with a double-purchase counterweight set: there the arbor travels half the distance of the batten and carries twice its weight.

Truss & Loads

Truss is rated by its manufacturer, for a given span and a given way of loading it. The chart is the rating — not how strong it looks.

Box truss

Four chords. The standard for touring rigs. 12″ and 20.5″ box are the common sizes.

Triangle / ladder

Three or two chords. Lighter, weaker, orientation matters. Ladder is not for heavy point loads.

Damage

Bent or dented chord or diagonal, cracked weld, damaged spigot or coupler — out of service. Aluminium is never straightened and reused.

Connections

Spigots, pins and R-clips (or bolts) on every connection. Every pin, every time. Don't mix manufacturers.

Reading a load chart

UDL

Uniformly distributed load — weight spread evenly along the span. Usually the biggest number on the chart.

Point loads

Centre point, third points, quarter points. A single load at mid-span is the worst case — much lower than the UDL.

Span

Distance between supports (motors). Longer span, lower allowable load — fast.

Flying truss

  1. Clear the floor under and around it. One voice calls the moves.
  2. Bump each motor until all chains are just taut — truss still on the floor.
  3. Lift a few inches and stop. Check level, check every point, check nothing is snagged.
  4. Fly to trim, keeping it level the whole way. A lagging motor racks the truss and overloads the others — stop and bump to level.

How much is at each motor

Each motor carries the weight in its tributary zone — half the distance to each neighbour — plus the motor, chain and anything hung right at the point. End motors only have half a zone on one side.

Example. 40′ truss on 4 evenly spaced motors with 20 lb/ft spread along it. Spacing is about 13.3′. Interior motors carry about 13.3′ × 20 = 267 lb of that load; end motors about 133 lb. Then add the motor, chain bag and cable at each point. Cable is the weight everyone underestimates.

Safety & Inspection

Most rigging injuries come from falls, dropped objects and gear that should have been tagged out. All three are preventable.

Calls everybody knows

“Headache!”

Something is falling. Don't look up — cover your head and move out from under the work.

“Rope!”

A line is being dropped from above. Clear the area under it.

“Heads!”

Something is moving overhead — a set flying in, a point going up. Look and stay clear.

“Motors running”

Chain motors are moving. Nobody under the rig; eyes on every point.

Fall protection

When

OSHA: 4 ft in general industry, 6 ft in construction, 10 ft on scaffolds. In the steel, you are tied off 100% of the time — including while moving.

The system (ABCs)

Anchorage rated 5,000 lb per person (or engineered), Body harness (full body, never a belt), Connector (lanyard or self-retracting lifeline).

Clearance

Free fall + lanyard deceleration + your height + a safety margin must fit above the next level down. A 6-ft lanyard needs far more than 6 ft below.

Suspension trauma

Hanging still in a harness can make you lose consciousness within minutes. There has to be a rescue plan before anyone goes up.

Tag it out — what retires gear

GearOut of service when

Build the Bridle

The head rigger hands you a point off the plot. Work out the leg lengths, the tension in each leg, and whether the hardware will hold it.

What it's drilling. Pythagoras for leg length, the closer leg carrying more, tension always beating the leg's share of the weight, the 120° limit, and checking hardware against the tension — not the load.
