How to use this

Read the reference tabs once, then live in the Simulator, Drills and Scenarios.

The idea underneath all of it: every mic and DI on stage is one signal that has to land on one numbered input. The input list is the law. Your job is to put the right mic in the right place, run its XLR to the right channel on the drum sub, label it, and fix it fast when line check finds a dead one. Gain, EQ and phantom switching belong to FOH — but you have to know which channels need phantom so you can tell them.

Where your job ends

Backline hand

Set the gear, place mics where the drum tech or A1 asks, run and label XLR, patch the drum sub to the input list, swap bad cables at line check.

Audio department

Console, gain, EQ, phantom, polarity flips, monitor mixes, system tuning. You tell them what is where; they decide what it sounds like.

Mic types

Dynamic no phantom

A tiny speaker in reverse. Takes enormous level without distorting. Kick out, snare, toms, guitar and bass amps. SM57, Beta 52A, e604, D112.

Condenser 48 V

More sensitive, more detail, and it needs phantom power from the console to work at all. Hi-hat, overheads, ride, acoustic. C451, SM81, C414.

Boundary 48 V

Lies flat on a surface. Inside the kick on a pillow or the shell. The Beta 91A is a condenser — it needs phantom, which surprises people because it lives in the kick.

Placement, piece by piece

  1. Kick in — boundary flat inside on the pillow or shell, pointed at the beater. Closer to the beater = more click.
  2. Kick out — at the port in the front head, angled toward the beater impact. In the port = more attack, further out = more boom.
  3. Snare top — about 1–2″ above the head, 2″ in from the rim, about 45°. Aim the back of the mic at the hi-hat to reject it.
  4. Snare bottom — under the drum aimed up at the wires. Faces the opposite way to the top mic, so FOH flips its polarity.
  5. Hi-hat — about 4″ above the top cymbal, between bell and edge, angled away from the snare. Keep the stand clear of the drummer's left foot and the hat clutch.
  6. Rack toms — clip to the rim, 1–2″ above the head, aimed toward the centre. Keep the capsule out of the stick path.
  7. Floor tom — clip or short boom from the side, 1–2″ up, aimed at the centre. Watch the drummer's right hand.
  8. Overheads — a matched pair, roughly 3–4 ft above the kit, both the same distance from the snare. Measure it with a tape or a mic cable. Unequal distance = a thin, phasey snare.
Ask before you guess. Drum techs and A1s have strong opinions about placement. Set to the standard, then ask "where do you want them?" before you lock anything down.

The Drum Sub (Veam Box)

A Veam box — also called a stage box, drop, sub-snake or drum sub — is a box of numbered XLR inputs that lives on or beside the riser. Every drum mic lands in it, and a single multipin carries the lot to the stage rack or FOH. Ten-plus XLRs become one cable.

Inputs are female, returns are male. A mic's male XLR plugs into the box. Returns send signal back out — to a drum-fill wedge or an in-ear pack — so they have the opposite gender.

How to cable a drum sub

  1. Get the input list. From the touring A1 or the house audio lead. Do not start without it.
  2. Find the box position. Usually on the riser, upstage of the drummer or at a riser corner, away from foot traffic.
  3. Place mics and DIs first. Confirm positions with the drum tech, then run cable — not the other way round.
  4. Run each XLR from the mic to the box. Excess coiled at the mic end, not in the middle of the run.
  5. Patch to the list. Channel number on the box = channel number on the list. Read it twice.
  6. Label the box end. Gaff and a Sharpie: 1 K-IN, 2 K-OUT, 3 SN-T. Label the mic end too if the run is long.
  7. Tell FOH which channels need phantom — every condenser and active DI. Then call ready for line check.
  8. Line check: scratch or tap each mic in order while FOH watches the meters. Dead channel? Seat both ends, swap the cable, then ask about phantom.
  9. Dress it. Tape runs flat to the riser, keep them away from the drummer's feet and pedals, keep the kit able to move if it needs to roll.
Labels win line check. When FOH says "nothing on 6" you want to be able to point at the cable on 6 in under a second — not trace it back through a nest of identical black XLR.

Instruments & DI

The gear behind the kit. For each one you need to know how its signal gets to the console — a mic, a DI, or both — and which channels it uses.

What a DI does

Direct Injection box

An instrument puts out an unbalanced, high-impedance signal on a ¼″ cable. That cannot travel far without hum and loss. A DI turns it into a balanced, low-impedance, mic-level signal on XLR that runs a hundred feet to the console cleanly.

Bass / keys→ ¼″ → DI IN→ THRU → amp (player still hears their rig)
DI→ XLR OUT → Drum sub / stage box→ Console

Passive DI

A transformer, no power needed. Rugged and simple. Great on bass and hot keyboard outputs. Radial JDI, ProDI.

Active DI 48 V

Electronics inside, powered by phantom or a battery. Better for weak or very high-impedance sources like piezo acoustic pickups. Radial J48, BSS AR-133.

Ground lift

A switch on the DI that breaks pin 1 at the XLR side. The first move on a hum. It never means lifting the safety ground on an amp's power cord — that one stays connected, always.

The instruments

Bass guitar

DI first, often an amp mic as well. The DI sits between the bass and the amp, using THRU to feed the amp. Bass rig is usually stage left of the kit.

Electric guitar

SM57 on the cab. 1–2″ off the grille, aimed at the cone: centre is brighter, toward the edge is warmer. Modelers and direct outs get a DI instead.

Keys & synths

Stereo — two DIs, two channels, always adjacent (L then R). Two keyboards = four channels. Patch only one side and half the instrument disappears.

Acoustic guitar

Pickup into a DI (after the player's pedals), or a small condenser about 6–8″ off the 12th fret. The input list says which.

XLR Runs

Running cable cleanly is the core physical skill of backline. A bad run is noise, hum, a dead channel, or a trip hazard.

Balanced: the same signal on pins 2 and 3 in opposite polarity. Noise picked up along the run lands on both equally, and the console takes the difference — the noise cancels. Phantom rides on pins 2 and 3 together.

Phantom power, 48 V

Needs it

Condensers (hi-hat, overheads, Beta 91A, room mics), active DIs without a battery.

Doesn't care

Dynamics — SM57, Beta 52A, e604. Phantom on or off, they work the same.

Careful

Ribbon mics, especially vintage ones, can be damaged by phantom if a cable is miswired or shorted. Flag every ribbon to FOH and let them decide.

Your role: you do not switch phantom — FOH does. But you know which channels need it, and you say so before line check, not after a condenser comes up dead.

Cable discipline

  • Coil over-under. Alternating loops lie flat and throw out straight. Coiling one way twists the cable and kills it over a season.
  • Excess at the mic end, never a loop in the middle of a walkway.
  • Lock the latch at the mic. A bump or a tug should not pull it out mid-show.
  • Seat both ends fully before you call ready. 80% in will pass line check and drop out in the second song.
  • Keep audio away from power — cross at 90° if you must, never run parallel to feeder, Soca or stingers.
  • No kinks, no tight bends. Gentle curves only.
  • Tape flat, ramp across traffic, and label both ends of long runs.

Troubleshoot fast

Dead channel

Seat both ends → swap to a known-good cable → condenser? ask about phantom → DI? check the ¼″ and the instrument's volume → move it to a spare channel to split mic/cable/input.

Cuts in and out

Wiggle each connector while FOH listens. If the fault follows the cable, bin the cable and tag it. If it stays on the channel, it's the input.

Hum or buzz

Ground lift on the DI first. Then get the cable away from power. Then put the amp on the same power as the audio system. Never lift the safety ground on a power cord.

Wrong source on a channel

A mis-patch. Trace it back, compare to the list, swap it. This is why both ends get labels.

Patch the Drum Sub

You get a real input list. Tap a mic, then the channel it belongs on. When it's patched, tell FOH which channels need phantom. Every show's list is different — read it, don't assume.
