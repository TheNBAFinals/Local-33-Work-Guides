The Four Numbers

Electric is not a memory job. It is four numbers and one relationship between them. Everything else on this page is a consequence.

Volts, amps, watts, ohms

Volts is pressure. Amps is flow. Watts is work being done. Ohms is resistance to flow.

The plumbing analogy holds all the way down: voltage is the water pressure in the pipe, current is how much water is actually moving, resistance is how narrow the pipe is, and wattage is how hard the wheel at the end is turning.

The one equation that pays your rent:

Watts ÷ Volts = Amps

You will do this in your head twenty times a day and never once write it down. At 120 V, a 1200 W hazer draws 10 A. A 575 W Source Four draws just under 5 A. Eight Source Fours is 38 A, which does not fit on a 20 A circuit and it is not close.

Why this is the whole job. Nobody on a call will ask you to define a volt. They will hand you a cart of fixtures and a distro and expect you to know, without pausing, how many of those fixtures fit on one circuit. That is one division problem. Everything that goes wrong in electric goes wrong because somebody didn't do it.

Single phase and three phase

Why 208 and not 240. The three legs peak at different moments, 120° apart. Two of them added together never reach double — they reach 120 × √3, which is 208. That √3 shows up in every three-phase calculation you will ever do, and it is the only piece of maths in electric that looks like maths.

Single phase

One hot leg and a neutral. 120 V. This is a wall outlet, a small distro, a house circuit. Simple, limited.

Three phase — 120/208 wye

Three hot legs (A, B, C), a shared neutral, and a ground. Each leg to neutral is 120 V. Any leg to any other leg is 208 V. This is what feeds a real show.

Why three phase exists

It moves three times the power down not much more copper. That is the entire reason. A 400 A three-phase service delivers around 144 kW; the same 400 A single phase delivers 48 kW.

Three-phase capacity, without the panic

The formula

Watts = Volts × Amps × √3   (√3 ≈ 1.732)

A 200 A, 120/208 V three-phase service: 208 × 200 × 1.732 = about 72 kW.

The shortcut that actually gets used on a call: forget √3 and think of it as three separate 120 V services of 200 A each. 200 A × 120 V = 24 kW per leg, three legs, 72 kW total. Same answer, no square roots, and it puts your head in exactly the right place — because your real job is not the total. It is keeping the three legs even.

Balance is the skill

Why an unbalanced service trips with capacity to spare

A breaker protects one leg. It has no idea what the other two are doing. Put 180 A on leg A and 20 A each on B and C, and you will trip a 200 A main while using 220 A of a 600 A capacity — roughly a third of what you paid for.

The rule of thumb: keep the legs within about 10% of each other. Read the distro meters, and if one leg is running away from the others, move circuits — not fixtures — until it settles.

The other reason it matters: an unbalanced wye pushes current down the neutral. The neutral is sized on the assumption that it mostly cancels out. A badly unbalanced system heats a conductor that has no breaker on it.

How balance actually happens in practice. You don't calculate it up front. You cable the rig sensibly, energise, read the meters, and move things. An experienced electric hand glances at three numbers and knows within a second whether the rig is happy. That glance is worth learning — the Phase Balance trainer in the Simulators tab is built to teach exactly it.

The Distribution Chain

Power gets from the building to a fixture through a fixed sequence of boxes. Learn the sequence and you can walk into any venue and read the rig in thirty seconds.

The chain never changes — only how many boxes are in it. A ballroom breakout might be service → distro → fixture. An arena might have four layers of sub-distro. Same sequence either way.

What each box actually does

Company switch — the building's offer

A permanently installed disconnect with lugs or cam-lok receptacles, usually rated 100 A to 800 A, three phase. It is the venue's official point of connection for a production.

Connecting to it is licensed work. In California that means a qualified person, and on most calls that person is a house electrician or a designated head. Your job is to know it exists, know its rating, and never be the one who opens it.

Distro — one big feed becomes many small ones

Takes the feeder in and gives you protected outputs: Socapex for six circuits at a time, plus individual Edison, stage pin or L6-20 outlets, each on its own breaker.

A good distro has per-leg metering on the front. Those three numbers are the most useful thing in the room and most people walk past them.

Sub-distro is the same idea one layer down: a smaller box fed from the main distro, placed near where the power is actually needed so you are not running fifty jumpers across a stage.

Dimmer rack vs relay rack — the distinction that kills fixtures

A dimmer rack varies voltage. A tungsten lamp responds to that by getting brighter or dimmer. The classic touring box is an ETC Sensor3 with D20 dimmer modules — two 20 A circuits per module, 2.4 kW each, controlled over DMX or network by a CEM3 control module.

A relay rack just switches circuits fully on or fully off. In a Sensor3 that is an R20 relay module dropped into the same slot as a dimmer. Same rack, same footprint, completely different behaviour.

Why you care: anything with a power supply — every LED fixture, every mover, every hazer, every media server — must go on constant power. A dimmer feeding an LED at 50% is delivering a chopped waveform to a supply that expects a clean sine. Best case it flickers and buzzes. Worst case you cook the supply and hand back a dead fixture.

This is why a rack full of R20s or a plain non-dim distro is now more common on a corporate call than a rack full of dimmers. The rig is mostly LED.

The question that identifies you as competent. When someone hands you a circuit and a fixture, ask “is that circuit dimmed or constant?” On a mixed rig it is the single most useful thing you can say, and it takes two seconds. People notice.

Generators

When the building isn't the source

Outdoor and festival work runs on diesel gensets, sized in kW or kVA. The same chain applies — genset replaces company switch, feeder runs to distro, everything downstream is identical.

What changes:

• Grounding is a real job. A genset has to be bonded and grounded properly, and that is not a task you take on unasked.
• Load steps matter. Bringing a large load on all at once can drag the engine down and dip the voltage. Bring things up in stages.
• Fuel and runtime are scheduling problems. Somebody has to know how long it runs and who refuels it. On a long day that becomes your problem faster than you expect.
• Audio hates generators. Cheap or overloaded gensets put noise on the line and it ends up in the PA. If audio starts chasing a hum, the generator is a legitimate suspect.

Feeder & Cam-Lok

Feeder is the heavy stuff between the service and the distro. It is the highest-current thing you will ever put your hands on, and the order you connect it in is not a preference.

The cable itself

Type W is the entertainment standard for portable feeder — thick rubber insulation on each conductor plus a heavy rubber jacket, built to be coiled, dragged, driven over and rained on. Type SOOW is similar with added oil resistance, common up to about 2/0.

Feeder is sold and rigged as single conductors, one colour per leg, not as a bundle. Five separate cables make one three-phase run.

The colour code

Hook up left to right: ground first, neutral second, then the hot legs in the order they sit on the distro you are actually tying into. Strike is the reverse, right to left: hot legs off, then neutral, then ground last. The reason is simple: at every intermediate step, the equipment must already have a path to ground. Black/red/blue tells you which leg is A, B or C — the panel layout, not the colour code, sets the order.

The connectors

Cam-Lok (Eaton Crouse-Hinds, E1016 series) and PowerLok (Hubbell) are the two dominant single-pole locking connectors in North American entertainment. They intermate, and most rental stock mixes them freely.

Each is colour-coded to one job. Green ground, white neutral, black/red/blue for phases A/B/C — that identifies the leg, it does not set the order you land them in. The colour is the safety system — it is why you can cable a 400 A service correctly in a dark loading dock.

Seat it and turn the collar. A connector that is pushed on but not locked will arc under feeder current, carbonise the contact face instantly, and destroy both halves. Pull-test every one.

Where the danger actually is. Feeder does not hurt people because someone forgot a formula. It hurts people because a connector was made up live, or made up in the wrong order, or half-seated and left. De-energise, lock out, then connect ground first. If you cannot confirm the source is dead and locked, you do not touch the connector — you find the person whose ticket it is.

Ampacity — rough working figures

What size carries what

Feeder is sized by ampacity (can it carry the current without overheating) and voltage drop (is the run long enough to lose meaningful voltage). Typical copper working figures:

• 2 AWG — around 130 A — sub-feeds to smaller distro
• 1/0 — around 170 A — medium runs
• 2/0 — around 195 A — the standard touring feeder
• 4/0 — around 260 A — arena main feeds

These are working figures, not code. Actual ampacity depends on insulation temperature rating, whether the cable is in free air or bundled, ambient temperature and how it is run. The person sizing the feeder is doing a real calculation. You are reading the number off the jacket and confirming it matches what the distro expects.

Long runs lose voltage. Past about 100 ft it starts to matter, and 3% is the usual target. Low voltage at the far end makes tungsten look amber and makes motors run hot.

Socapex

Six circuits down one cable. The single most common thing you will coil, run, dress and troubleshoot on an electric call.

The connector face has no useful pattern to it, so do not try to memorise pin positions — memorise the grouping: odd = hot, even = neutral, teens = ground. Circuit number × 2 gives you its neutral pin, minus 1 gives you its hot, and 12 + circuit gives you its ground.

Breakout / fan-out

19-pin in, six individual outlets out — Edison, stage pin or L6-20 depending on the stock. This is where the multi becomes something a fixture can plug into.

Thru box

A breakout with a 19-pin male output as well, so you can carry the remaining circuits further down the truss and break out again. Terminating boxes have no thru.

Why six circuits

Six 20 A circuits down one cable instead of six cables. On a truss with 24 circuits that is four cables instead of twenty-four. The entire point is cable management.

How Socapex actually fails

• Bent or pushed-back pin — one dead circuit, everything else fine. Look into the connector face before you blame the dimmer.
• Broken conductor at the strain relief — the cable flexes most where it enters the connector. Intermittent circuits that come and go when the cable is moved are almost always this.
• Contamination — outdoor shows, rain and dust. Intermittent everything.
• Reversed polarity — hot and neutral swapped in one circuit during a repair. The fixture works normally. But the lamp socket shell sits at line voltage even with the dimmer at zero, and the person who finds out is whoever re-lamps it. This is why unfamiliar multis get tested before they go in a rig, not after.

Coiling is a skill and people watch you do it. Over-under, every time, no exceptions. A Socapex that has been coiled wrong for a year has broken conductors inside it and nobody knows which circuit will die next. How you handle cable is the fastest read anyone gets on whether you have done this before.

Connectors

The connector is the safety system. It exists so that a 208 V outlet physically cannot accept a 120 V plug. Learn what each body means and you stop having to trust labels.

The one that costs money: L5-20 vs L6-20. Both are twist-lock. Both are 20 A. Both are three-prong. From two feet away in a dim room they look like the same connector. L5-20 is 120 V. L6-20 is 208 V. Put a 120 V fixture on an L6-20 and you do not trip a breaker — you send double voltage into its power supply and it dies on the spot, usually with a smell. This is the single most expensive mistake available to you on an electric call.
The structural difference: L5-20 has a hot, a neutral and a ground. L6-20 has two hots and a ground — no neutral at all, because 208 V comes from the difference between two legs. That is why the blade geometry differs, and why the two will not mate. If it will not go in, that is the connector doing its job. Do not find an adapter.

The full set

Edison — NEMA 5-15 / 5-20

120 V, 15 A or 20 A. The household plug. On a 5-20 the neutral blade is T-shaped so a 20 A device cannot go into a 15 A outlet.

Where you meet it: practicals, small LED fixtures, laptops, chargers, and every breakout that ends in a duplex. No locking — it falls out, which is why it gets taped or tied off overhead.

Stage pin — 2P&G

120 V, 20 A. Three flat pins in a line in a fibre body. The theatre connector, and still standard on conventionals and dimmer circuits.

The known failure: the pins spread with age and lose contact pressure, which causes heat and intermittent fixtures. A pin spreader is a real tool and the fix is to spread the male pins slightly.

L5-20 twist-lock

120 V, 20 A. Curved blades, push and twist to lock. It stays in when the cable is pulled, which is the entire reason it exists.

Where you meet it: overhead runs, truss circuits, anything where a dropped Edison would be a problem.

L6-20 twist-lock

208 V, 20 A. Two hots and a ground. Same size shell as an L5-20 and this is exactly why people get hurt by it.

Where you meet it: large moving heads, motor controllers, some racks and processors. If a fixture spec says 208 V, expect this or a True1.

powerCON — blue in, grey out

Neutrik, up to 20 A. Twist-locking, and the two halves are keyed and colour-coded: blue is power in, grey is power out. You physically cannot chain them backwards.

The limitation: original powerCON is not rated to be connected or disconnected under load. Kill the circuit first.

powerCON TRUE1 / TRUE1 TOP

Up to 16 A, IP65 when mated. The upgrade: rated for connect and disconnect under load, sealed, and the standard input on modern LED fixtures and movers.

TRUE1 TOP is the current revision — it will mate with older TRUE1 panel connectors but the sealing and retention are improved. If someone says “true one” they mean this family.

IEC 60309 — “pin and sleeve”

Round industrial connectors, colour-coded by voltage: blue is 120 V, red is three-phase 208 V. Common on sub-distro feeds, festival power and anything imported.

Rated 16 A up to 125 A. Weather-sealed and very hard to mis-mate, which is why outdoor events like them.

19-pin Socapex

Six 20 A circuits in one round multi-pin. Not a fixture connector — it goes rack to truss, and a breakout turns it into something a fixture can use.

Cam-Lok / PowerLok E1016

Single pole, up to 400 A. One conductor per connector, colour-coded green / white / black / red / blue. Feeder only. Never energised while being made up.

The rule that covers all of it

The connector tells you the voltage. Believe the connector, not the label.

Tape falls off. Labels get reused from the last show. Somebody's Sharpie says “208” on a circuit that was re-fed this morning. The connector body is the one piece of information that cannot be wrong, because it is physically shaped by the voltage it carries.

The corollary: the moment someone offers you an adapter that bridges two different connector types, stop and ask why. Legitimate adapters exist — Edison to stage pin, 5-20 to L5-20 — and they are all same-voltage conversions. An adapter that crosses 120 V to 208 V is not an adapter, it is a fixture-killer someone built in a shop.

Drill this until it is instant. The Connector ID drill in the Simulators tab shows you a body and asks for voltage, amperage and what belongs on it. On a real call nobody gives you time to think about it — you are handed a cable in a dark room and expected to know.

Racks, Distros & Boxes

The gear you will actually be handed. Names matter here — a head will say “grab a Bento” or “that's coming off the Sensor” and expect you to know what is in front of you.

Dimmer and relay racks

ETC Sensor3 — the one you will meet

The dominant touring and installed rack in North America. A single Sensor3 takes up to 48 modules, and because each module holds two circuits, that is up to 96 channels of 2.4 kW dimming in one rack. Modules slide in and out by hand, no tools.

The modules, and this is the part that matters:

• D20 — dimmer module. Two 20 A dimmed circuits. Tungsten only.
• R20 — relay module. Two 20 A circuits that switch fully on or off, constant power. Drops into the same slot as a D20 with no other change to the rack.
• TP20 — provides unregulated constant power to individual circuits.

Why this is worth knowing: when a rig turns out to be mostly LED, the fix is not a new rack — it is pulling D20s and dropping in R20s. If you understand that, you understand why a head might ask how many relay modules are in the rack before they let an LED anywhere near it.

CEM3 is the brain — the control module that handles DMX, network, patch and monitoring for the whole rack. It is also where you find out which circuits are actually reporting a load, which is a diagnostic worth asking about.

Portable formats: touring packs and racks in 24- and 48-module configurations, in road cases with cam-lok in and Socapex out.

How a rack gets fed and what comes out

In: feeder on cam-lok, three phase. Out: Socapex, usually six circuits per connector, sometimes with a few individual outlets on the front for local needs.

Phasing inside the rack is not random. Modules are grouped so that consecutive circuits land on different legs — the rack is doing part of your balancing work for you by design. This is also why pulling all your circuits from one corner of a rack is a worse idea than spreading them out.

Distros

Lex PowerHOUSE — the workhorse portable distro

A typical 100 A PowerHOUSE converts a 100 A three-phase 120/208 V feed into twelve NEMA 5-20 duplex receptacles and two Socapex outputs. That single sentence describes the majority of corporate and mid-size event power you will touch.

4Wall and most LA rental houses stock these. If you are told “there's a hundred-amp distro at stage left,” picture this box.

Lex PowerRACK is the rack-mount version of the same idea, for road cases and permanent installs.

Lex Bento Box — small, stackable, everywhere

A family of compact distribution boxes in an impact-resistant moulded housing. Common configurations:

• 30 A Bento — six Edison duplex outlets. The one that appears at every uplight position and every catering table.
• 100 A Lunchbox — 5-20 duplex receptacles, collapsible handle, mounts vertical or horizontal.
• Doghouse — end-plate grips for handling in tight spaces.

The design intent is that several of them chain from one source, so you get multiple power positions around a room off a single feed. That is the thing to internalise — not the model names, but the pattern of one feed becoming several local drop points.

Motion Labs

The other name you will hear in touring distro and motor control — distribution boxes, Socapex distro, and hoist control. If a rig has chain motors, Motion Labs gear is a reasonable bet for what is controlling them.

Spider boxes — the term means two things

This one genuinely confuses people, so know both. Some crews call the fan-out itself a spider — a Socapex head with six cable legs hanging off it, which does look like a spider. Other crews call a small portable distro box a spider box — a box with a feed in and a spread of Edisons out. When someone says “grab a spider,” look at what they are pointing at. Both are correct usage and neither camp thinks the other one exists.

Breakout types and when to use which

Fan-out / spider

19-pin head, six cable legs, connectors on the ends. No box.

Use it when the six fixtures are spread out — the legs reach different positions on their own and you do not need jumpers.

Downside: the legs are a fixed length. If they are too short you are stuck, and if they are too long you have six tails to dress.

Break-in / reverse fan

The opposite — six individual inputs collapsing into one 19-pin.

Use it when your source is individual circuits (a wall of Edisons, a small distro) but your run needs to be a single multi. Common when you are feeding a truss from a house panel rather than a rack.

Terminating breakout box

19-pin female in, six outlets on the box, nothing else.

Use it when this is the end of the line and all six circuits land right here — a truss corner, a lighting position, a dimmer beach.

Advantage over a fan-out: the outlets are on a solid box you can tie to truss, so nothing dangles and jumper length is your choice.

Thru breakout box

19-pin in, six outlets, plus a 19-pin male THRU out.

Use it when you want to tap circuits at one position and carry the rest further down the truss. Chain several and one Socapex run serves the whole length.

The catch: the circuits are still the same six. A thru box does not create more — it just moves where you can reach them. Two boxes on one multi means those two boxes are sharing six circuits, and if both are fully loaded you have doubled up on breakers.

Socapex jumper / extension

19-pin male to 19-pin female. Just length.

Use it when the run is longer than the multi you have. Prefer one long multi to three jumpered together — every connection is another place a pin can back out.

Adapters — the honest version

Edison to stage pin, stage pin to L5-20, Edison to L5-20. All fine — same voltage, same amperage, just different bodies.

What is not fine: anything crossing 120 V to 208 V, and anything that lets a 20 A device sit on a 15 A circuit without you knowing. If an adapter surprises you, ask before you use it.

Choosing between them on a real call

The decision, in order

1. Where do the six circuits need to land? All in one place → terminating box. Spread down a truss → thru boxes. Spread in different directions → fan-out.

2. Can I tie it off? A box on truss is better than a fan-out dangling by its legs. Overhead, this decides it.

3. How many circuits do I actually need here? If a position needs two circuits, do not land a whole multi on it — run a thru and take the other four somewhere useful.

4. What connector do the fixtures want? There is no point landing a stage-pin breakout in front of a truss full of True1 movers.

Most of the time somebody has already decided all four of these and the answer is on the plot. Reading the plot before you start pulling cable is the whole trick — and it is the difference between one trip to the cable cart and four.

Cam-Lok Sequence

The highest-stakes ten seconds in the department. Make the source safe, land ground then neutral, then the hot legs the way this distro is laid out — then break it down. Get it wrong and you will be told exactly what would have happened.

What it's drilling. Left to right on the way in — ground, neutral, then the hot legs as they sit on the panel — and right to left on the way out: hots, then neutral, then ground last. The hot-leg layout changes every run, so read the distro instead of memorising colours. Plus the two steps most people skip entirely — killing the source and locking it out before anything is touched, and verifying it is actually dead. The order is the safety system.

Phase Balance

Three legs, one rig. Spread the load evenly or trip a main with capacity to spare. Read the meters the way an experienced hand reads them — at a glance.

What it's drilling. Watts to amps in your head, the 10% balance target, why a breaker only sees its own leg, and the habit of moving circuits rather than fixtures. The Auto-balance button will solve it for you — use it to check your answer, not to skip the thinking.

Socapex Fault Finder

A circuit is dead on a truss. You have a rack, a multi, a breakout and six fixtures. Find the fault in the order that costs the least time.

What it's drilling. Diagnosis by pattern rather than by guessing. One dead circuit and one dead breakout are different faults with different causes, and the pattern tells you which before you climb anything. Every check costs time — the score is how much you spent.

Connector ID

A body appears. Name its voltage, its amperage and what belongs on it. No time to think — that is the point.

Why this drill and not a photo quiz. Photographs of connectors are worse than drawings for learning, because a photo shows you one specific dirty example under one specific light. A drawing shows you the geometry that never changes — blade angle, pin count, keyway position. That is what you actually recognise in a dark loading dock.

Safety & Practice

Electric is the department where being wrong stops being embarrassing and starts being dangerous. This page is short on purpose — everything on it is non-negotiable.

Know where your ticket ends. Tie-ins, anything behind a panel cover, and generator grounding are licensed work. In California that means a qualified person and it is not you unless someone has said so explicitly and you hold the ticket. Nobody has ever been thought less of for asking. People have absolutely been thought less of for finding out the hard way.

Lock out, tag out

The sequence

De-energise. Lock it. Tag it. Verify it is dead. Then work.

• Your lock, your key. The whole point is that only you can restore power to the thing you have your hands in. A lock somebody else can remove is decoration.
• Verify with a meter, and test the meter on a known live source first and again afterwards. A dead meter reads zero on a live conductor and that is how people die confident.
• One person removes their own lock. Not a favour you do for someone who has gone to lunch.

Arc flash

What actually hurts people at the service end

Shock is the risk everyone thinks about. At 400 A service level the bigger risk is arc flash — a fault that turns metal to vapour and throws heat and pressure outward, faster than anyone reacts.

What causes it in our world: a cam-lok made up live, a half-seated connector under load, a dropped tool across bus, moisture in a connection. All of them avoidable, all of them a sequence error rather than a knowledge gap.

This is why the connection order exists. Ground first, phases last, source dead and locked. It is not ceremony — it is the sequence that keeps you from being the path or the trigger.

The things that actually happen on a call

A breaker trips

Do not just reset it. A breaker that tripped did its job. Reset without finding out why and you will trip it again mid-show, or you will not, which is worse.

Ask: what changed? Something was added, something moved, or something failed. Add up the load on that circuit before you touch the handle.

Something smells hot

Trust your nose immediately. Hot plastic and ozone are real signals. Find it before you carry on. Usual suspects: a loose stage-pin connection, a spread pin, an over-loaded jumper, a connector not fully seated.

Warm is normal. Too hot to hold is not.

Cable on the floor

Anything crossing a walkway gets ramped or matted. Anything in a fire lane or doorway is a code issue, not a preference. Anything a forklift crosses gets real cable protection.

Tape is for dressing, not for protection. Tape over a cable in a doorway satisfies nobody except the person who applied it.

Water and outdoors

Connections get raised off the ground, covered, or both. A cam-lok sitting in a puddle is a fault waiting for a schedule. GFCI on anything wet-location, and test it — the test button exists to be pressed.

Working overhead

Everything gets a safety — fixtures, breakouts, boxes hung on truss. Anything you can drop, you tie off.

Nothing loose in your pockets over people. Hard hat area means hard hat, including for you.

Handing over

If you leave a circuit dead, a cable pulled or a breaker off, tell someone and label it. Half the problems on a second day of load-in are yesterday's unfinished work that nobody wrote down.

Habits that mark you as experienced

None of these are difficult. All of them get noticed.

• Over-under, always. How you coil is the fastest read anyone gets on you.
• Label as you go, not at the end. At the end you will not remember.
• Dress cable as you run it. Coming back to tidy is a job that never happens.
• Read the meters when you energise, and again after the rig is fully up. Two glances, and you will catch an imbalance before it catches you.
• Leave service loops. Cable pulled dead tight is cable that gets damaged when anything moves.
• Coil it the way you want to find it. The next person is often you at 2 a.m.
• Ask about the circuit before you plug the fixture in. Dimmed or constant. Two seconds.

The one that matters most. If you are not sure, stop and ask. On this department the cost of asking is ten seconds of mild awkwardness. The cost of guessing is a dead fixture, a dark show, or a person. Every good head would rather answer a question than fix a mistake, and the ones who make you feel bad for asking are telling you something useful about themselves.
