Cables & Connectors

Know what each carries and how far it goes. That alone solves half the problems on a floor.

XLR Analog Audio

The most common cable in audio. Three pins: Pin 1 = shield/ground, Pin 2 = positive/hot, Pin 3 = negative/cold.

The rule that never changes: female receives, male sends. So the female end goes into the source device's output, the male end goes into the next device's input. Balanced — that's why it rejects noise over long runs.

Tell them apart instantly: solid pins sticking out = male. Holes = female. Mnemonic that works and you won't forget — pins go in holes.

Coax (BNC) RF

Looks identical to SDI — same cable, same connector. The difference is what's on it. Coax in the audio world carries RF from your paddle antennas to your receivers (or to an antenna distro).

Because it looks like SDI, label it. This is the #1 cable people grab wrong on a mixed AV cart.

SDI (BNC) Video

Coax cable with a BNC connector carrying video. Runs about 300 feet before signal degradation. Locks on with a twist — it doesn't fall out, which is why it's the standard for anything crossing a room.

The most important figure in this guide. Same cable, same BNC connector, same twist-lock. The only thing that differs is the signal riding on it. Trace the run or read the label — never trust your eyes on this one.

HDMI Video + Audio

Carries both video and audio. Maxes out around 50 feet before degradation — that's the whole reason converters exist.

Friction-fit connector. It walks out of ports. Tape it or use a locking adapter on anything that matters.

Shape is the tell: a lopsided trapezoid — flat on top, tapered underneath. It only goes in one way, and it never locks. Every other connector in this guide either screws, twists, or clips.

IEC (D-Plug) Power

Supplies power to active speakers and other powered gear. If a powered speaker is silent, this is the first thing you look at — before you look at anything clever.

CAT / Ethernet Data

Data between network switches, receivers, and computers. Also the backbone for Dante audio networking.

Confusable at a glance in a dark room. IEC has a notched, D-shaped body with three flat slots. RJ45 is smaller, has a springy clip on top, and shows eight gold contacts.

Snake Multi-channel Audio

Multiple XLR runs bundled into one jacket. Purpose: condense the number of cable runs. One snake from stage to front of house instead of sixteen loose XLRs across a walkway.

The lookalike trap: Coax and SDI are physically the same cable with the same BNC ends. One is carrying RF to a receiver, the other is carrying video to a display. Label both ends of every run — the deck says it and every A2 who's been burned says it louder.

Microphones

Dynamic

Rugged, hard to overload, no external power needed. Better for vocals in live sound. Most common mic in corporate settings. Works best in live environments.

Condenser

Significantly better sound quality and far more sensitive — picks up quiet sources. Common on high-frequency instruments and vocals. Works best in a studio environment. Needs power.

Lavalier ("Lav") Most-used in AV

The most common piece of mic equipment in corporate AV. Normally a condenser. Wins on size and on picking up clear vocals from the presenter without being in the shot.

Silhouette is the giveaway. Dynamic = round ball grille on a tapered handle. Condenser = straight body with a large mesh basket, often side-address. Lav = a capsule the size of a pencil eraser on a thin cable ending in a pack connector.

SM57 vs. SM58 the two you'll see most

Both are dynamics. Both are on nearly every truck. They are not interchangeable.

SM58 — the ball grille one. Frequency response shaped specifically for vocals. This is the most popular microphone in the world and it's what goes on a podium or in a hand.

SM57 — the small flat-nosed one. Flatter response, small grille. Built for instruments — drums, guitar cabs. The narrow head lets you get it in close where a 58 won't fit.

One glance tells you which. Big round ball = 58 = voices. Small flat nose = 57 = instruments. Handing a presenter a 57 isn't wrong exactly, but anyone who knows will notice.

Inside a dynamic mic

Worth knowing because it explains why a dynamic behaves the way it does: windscreen → diaphragm → coil → magnetic core → capsule → transformer → XLR output.

Sound waves move the diaphragm, which moves a coil inside a magnet, which generates a tiny voltage. The transformer boosts it enough to reach the console. It's a loudspeaker running backwards — same induction coil, opposite direction.

That's why it needs no external power (it generates its own signal) and why it takes abuse (there's nothing delicate or electrically charged in the path).

Dynamic — the tradeoffs

Wins: hard to overload, rugged, better for live vocals, no external power, corporate default.

Costs: less sensitive — needs a louder source. Not well suited to high frequencies.

Condenser — the tradeoffs

Wins: better sound quality, far more sensitive, great on high-frequency sources, studio-grade detail.

Costs: needs phantom power, distorts more easily if misused, far more expensive. Its sensitivity makes it impractical in most corporate rooms.

Phantom power (+48V) travels on XLR

XLR doesn't just carry audio — it can carry 48 volts of power back up the same cable. That's how a condenser gets fed.

The practical version: condenser needs it, dynamic doesn't. A dead condenser is very often just phantom left off on that channel. Look there before you swap the mic.

Polar Patterns

A polar pattern is the shape of what a mic hears. Pick wrong and you're amplifying the room instead of the presenter — which is the same thing as inviting feedback.

The shaded area is what the mic hears. Tighter pattern = more rejection of the room = more gain before feedback. That's the whole reason this matters on a live floor.
Most lavs are cardioid. That's deliberate — they reject sound from the back and sides, so they pick up the presenter and not the room, the speakers, or the guy talking at the next table. It's also why lav placement matters so much: point the pattern at the mouth, not the shoulder.

Micing a presenter

Wardrobe decides your job. A button-up shirt gives you easy access to good placement. A sweatshirt or a dress does not — plan for extra time, tape, and a conversation.

Wireless & RF

Mic→ Transmitter≈ RF ≈ Antenna→ Receiver→ XLR → Mixer

Transmitter

Every wireless mic has two halves. The transmitter takes the electrical signal from the mic and converts it into a radio frequency. Bodypack (like a ULXD1) for a lav, or built into a handheld.

Receiver

Takes in the encoded RF and turns it back into analog signal. That signal then goes to the mixer over XLR — female end into the receiver, male end into the mixer.

The two meters on the receiver face are your whole job as an A2. RF meter = is the radio link healthy. Audio meter = is sound actually arriving. RF good but audio dead means a muted pack or a bad lav — not a frequency problem.

Paddle antennas

Replace the little whip antennas on the back of a receiver. 200–300 ft of pickup vs. about 75 ft on whips. Get them up, get them out front, keep bodies and metal out of the path.

Unmistakable shape. A paddle is a flat directional arrowhead on a mic stand — you aim the wide end at the stage. A whip is a bare rod screwed straight into the receiver. If you see whips on a big room, that's your dropout problem.

Antenna distro

Capture with two paddles, then distribute that signal to every receiver over coax. Instead of eight receivers each fighting with their own whips, they all share two well-placed paddles.

Frequency coordinator

On big events there's often a frequency coordinator who assigns you frequencies based on the strongest open RF in the building — because every department is competing for the same spectrum.

When there isn't one, you assign them yourself using the receiver's scan function. Know both situations: on a large show, don't just scan and take whatever you find — ask whether someone is coordinating first. Taking a frequency that belongs to another department is a fast way to make enemies and kill someone else's mic.

Setting frequencies — the sequence

  1. Connect your antennas and power up the receiver. Antennas first — scanning without them gives you a garbage result.
  2. Hit the scan function. Choose whether you're scanning the group or the channel.
  3. When it finds an open frequency, push Enter to confirm and set it.
  4. Sync the mic: push Sync on the receiver, hold the transmitter about 6 inches from the IR window.

Audio Gear

Digital mixer

Judged on: channel count, quality, EQ types (parametric and graphic), routing/AUX sends, digital vs. analog, phantom power, and application. Recallable scenes and a visual display are why pros prefer them.

Analog mixer

Looks harder, is actually simpler for beginners — every function has one knob. Experienced engineers dislike them for real reasons: size, limited EQ bands, limited routing, and no visual display.

Call it from ten feet away: a big screen and a sparse surface means digital. A dense grid of knobs with no display means analog. Analog looks harder but it's simpler — every function has exactly one physical control, and nothing is buried in a menu.

DI box

Takes unbalanced, high-impedance signal and converts it to balanced, low-impedance mic level. That's the whole job — it makes a signal safe to run long distances into a mixer.

PC DI box

Same purpose, built for laptops. Has a mini stereo (3.5mm) cable attached that converts to XLR. This is your presenter's MacBook getting into the console.

Stage box

Takes a group of signals and sends them down a snake, usually to front of house.

Rio Dante

Same function as a stage box, but passes signal over ethernet using Dante instead of a copper snake.

Dante

Digital Audio Network Through Ethernet. Creates a network where any compatible device can see and talk to any other. One CAT cable replaces a snake.

Speakers

A good speaker is measured by its ability to accurately reproduce 20 Hz – 20 kHz and to limit distortion at high volume. Powered speakers need IEC.

Signal Flow & Processing

Input Source→ Input Gain→ EQ→ Channel Level→ Mixer Output→ FOH Speaker

Channel strip

A single vertical unit on the console combining the mic preamp, EQ, compressor, and sometimes a gate — everything you need to shape one channel.

Gain structure

The goal: highest output level without feedback or noise.

The technique: set your fader to unity first, then slowly bring gain (trim) up until you're at the right volume. Doing it the other way — cranking the fader to chase a weak input — is how you end up with a noise floor you can't fix later.

The full procedure, in order:
  1. Input gain and channel fader all the way down. Start from nothing.
  2. Bring the master fader up to unity (unity = zero on the mixer).
  3. Slowly bring the individual channel fader to unity — while talking into the mic.
  4. Once the channel is at unity, adjust input gain to reach the level you want.

Faders get set to a known reference. Gain is the knob that does the actual work. If you're reaching for the fader to fix volume, you skipped a step.

You're living between the noise floor and clipping. That gap is your dynamic range; the space above nominal is your headroom.

Why the order matters: chasing a weak input with the fader lifts the noise floor along with the signal. Setting trim properly raises the signal above the hiss. Same apparent volume, completely different mix — and only one of them survives a full room.

EQ

Boosts some frequencies and cuts others. Two jobs: get the best sound from the source, and eliminate the frequencies causing feedback.

Corporate technique: don't excessively cut unless you're killing feedback. The target is natural — a CEO should sound like himself, not like a rock show.

More inputs doesn't mean new rules. You can fill every channel your mixer has and the principles don't change: source → path → processing → destination. Scale changes, flow doesn't.

Video Gear

Video switcher Chooses

Multiple inputs in, you choose which one goes out. One or more outputs depending on size. ATEM Mini Pro is the common small-format box.

Know which mode it's in. The ATEM Mini Pro ships in cut bus mode — press a source and it is on air immediately. In program/preview mode (set in ATEM Software Control) a button only loads preview until you hit CUT or AUTO.

SDI splitter / distribution Copies

Takes one signal and splits it to multiple outputs. Critical limitation: it can only split ONE signal, and every output shows the same content.

This is the one that gets people on a floor. If a client asks for different content on two screens and all you have is a splitter, the answer is no — and no amount of configuring changes that.
Switcher vs. splitter — the distinction people blow in interviews and on floors:
A switcher takes many and picks one. A splitter takes one and copies it to many. If a client wants different content on two screens, a splitter cannot do it. Ever.

Converters

Change a video signal from one format to another — usually SDI ↔ HDMI. Some also do HDMI → HDMI while changing frame rate and/or resolution (that's scaling).

The classic run: HDMI out of the switcher → converter → SDI across the room → second converter → HDMI into the display. Two converters, because HDMI can't make the trip.

Common boxes: Blackmagic Micro Converter (SDI↔HDMI), Decimator MD-LX (bi-directional).

Multiview monitor

Any standard monitor plugged into the switcher's multiview output. Lets the operator see every feed at once and choose which becomes the program.

TVs / monitors

Usually in addition to fast-fold screens — showing different content like live camera, or acting as confidence monitors so the presenter can see their own slides without turning around.

Fast-fold screens

Range of sizes, front or rear projection surface. Minimum two people to set, and normally set with a dress kit.

Projectors

Placed in front of or behind the fast-fold depending on the setup. Type varies; the job doesn't — take video signal, put it on the screen.

How you spot a converter on a cart: a small metal brick with two different connector types — round BNC on one end, flat HDMI on the other. That mismatch is the whole identity of the device. If both ends are the same, it's a splitter or a repeater, not a converter.

Video Signal Flow

Sources→ HDMI → Switcher→ HDMI → Converter→ SDI (long run) → Converter→ HDMI → Display

Sources are laptops, cameras, playback machines — anything generating content. Everything in the middle is just getting it there intact. You convert to SDI to survive the distance, then convert back because the display speaks HDMI.

Why two converters? HDMI dies around 50 ft. SDI goes 300. The converters are a translation layer for the long walk across the ballroom — not processing, just transport.

Datavideo Meeting-Room Switcher Rack

A cased rental package you'll meet in corporate breakouts: an 8-input switching rack, a touchscreen controller and a 16″ multiview monitor. Mixed SDI and HDMI in and out, scaling to 1080p, and two still stores for a holding slide.

Inputs

SDI 1–4 and HDMI 1–4. By default SDI 1–4 are Inputs 1–4 and HDMI 1–4 are Inputs 5–8 on the touchscreen.

Outputs

SDI 1–3 and HDMI 1–3. By default every output carries Program except HDMI 3, which is the multiview — that's where the 16″ monitor goes.

Controller

One CAT6 cable from the controller's DVIP port to the rack's PoE port carries both power and control. Not the RS-232 port.

Power-up, in this order

  1. Controller to the rack: CAT6 from the controller's DVIP port to the rack's Ethernet (PoE) port.
  2. 16″ monitor to HDMI OUT 3, and to power.
  3. Power to the rack, then the rack power supply's switch on the front. The controller boots on its own.
  4. Switcher on: press and hold its front power button for a second or two.
  5. Sources into the inputs, destinations off the outputs.

Clear the last show before you start

Racks come back from the last job with its routing, stills and names still in them. On the touchscreen: Settings (gear) → Load Default Settings → Basic Mode Routing Default, confirm, let it reboot. Then do the same with Advanced Mode Routing Default.

The touchscreen tabs

Switching

Where you run the show. In Basic mode this is also where you lock the screen so a client can only switch.

Cross Point

Maps physical inputs to switcher buttons. Presenter laptop on HDMI 1? Map it to Input 1 so it's the first button. Affects both modes.

Icon

Puts a label icon on a button — e.g. LOGO on the holding slide. Long-press to clear it back to “INPUT X”.

Still

Put the source in Program, go to Still, pick Still 1 or 2, press Grab, wait about 10 seconds. Stills only come from a live grab — no loading from USB.

Pick an output, then pick what feeds it. Basic mode only offers Program and Multiview; Advanced offers everything.

Audio Room — Set Order

This order exists so you never have to move something you already set. Follow it and you don't backtrack.

  1. Consider placement for your tech table and speakers based on the plot, or on preference if there's no plot.
  2. Separate the gear. Starting with speakers, put them in place.
  3. Set the tech table — receivers and mixer first.
  4. Power the receivers and connect the antennas. Then plug them into the mixer, in the channel you want them to appear.
  5. Connect XLR: female into the receiver, male into the mixer. The hardline shows up in that channel number.
  6. Scan and set frequencies, then sync your mics.
  7. Verify signal on the console. Confirm the channel you plugged into is actually receiving before you go any further. Don't assume — look at the meter.
  8. Power your speakers and connect them to the console with XLR. Two mains go to Main L/R based on placement; more than two speakers, or a recording feed, comes off AUX sends.
  9. Set gain structure — everything down, master to unity, channel fader to unity while talking, then gain. Make noise.
Step 8 is the one people skip. Verifying signal at the console before moving on means that when something breaks later, you already know the front half of the chain was good. Skip it and every future problem has twice the search space.

The five-step version to keep in your head

  • Set your speakers in place
  • Set your tech table
  • Power your speakers and tech table
  • Set your receivers and get signal to your mixer
  • Slowly bring your fader to unity, then set your gain structure
KEEP IT SIMPLE. There's a lot to think about — where gear goes, how long a cable you need, how to be efficient. The single most useful principle when you're standing in an empty room deciding where to start is to not over-engineer it. The simplest system that does the job is the one that fails least and strikes fastest.

Video Room — Set Order

  1. Place your monitors or projector and screen first.
  2. Set the tech table with your input sources and switcher.
  3. Connect the switcher to a converter/splitter if you have one, then run cable out to your displays.
  4. Test your signal by sending content out to every display. Not one. Every one.
Both rooms follow the same shape: destination first (speakers / screens), then the brain (mixer / switcher), then the path between them, then test. You place the thing you can't easily move, and work backward to the thing you control.

Best Practices

  • Double-check your gear before you set it — nothing broken, nothing missing
  • Make sure power is easily accessible
  • Find the cleanest cable path possible
  • Label both ends of every run so you can find the send you need instantly
  • Factory reset the mixer and label channels to match your inputs
  • Don't rush the system. Take your time and make it right the first time — a fast wrong setup costs more than a slow right one

What the Qu-16 does NOT have read this first

Knowing the gaps is more useful than knowing the features, because it tells you when advice you read online won't work on your desk.

Gain Structure on a Qu

What the meter is actually telling you

0 dB on the meter = -18 dBFS = +4 dBu at the XLR out. When the meter reads 0, you still have 18 dB of headroom. Zero is not the limit — it's the target.

Pk LED lights at -3 dBFS. Signal LED (the lowest one) lights at -48 dBFS — if that isn't lighting, you have no signal, so check the patch before you touch gain.

  1. Kill the output first. LR and mix masters down, or amps down. You're about to make loud mistakes on purpose.
  2. PAFL the channel. Now you're metering that channel, not the mix.
  3. Set pad and phantom BEFORE gain. 48V on for condensers and active DIs; off for dynamics and most wireless receivers. -20 dB pad for anything hot. Do this first and you only set gain once.
  4. Make the source do its loudest realistic thing. A paragraph at real volume and real distance — not a polite "check one two." Everyone gets louder with an audience.
  5. Gain up until the meter averages 0 and peaks +6 to +9. For speech in a live room, err low — averaging -6 to 0 is safer, because you're fighting feedback, not noise.
  6. Set the HPF now, not later. It cleans the meters and buys back headroom immediately.
  7. Drop PAFL, fader to unity, build the mix. If a fader ends up at -25 or +10, your gain is wrong — go back to step 5.
The rule: gain sets how much signal exists. Faders set how much of it you want. If you're riding gain during a show, you've confused the two.

Processing

HPF — the highest-value control on the desk, and it's free

12 dB/octave, sweepable 20 Hz to 2 kHz. Roll off what the source doesn't produce. Male speech 80–100 Hz · female speech 100–120 Hz · lav 100–150 Hz · headset 120 Hz · boundary 120–150 Hz · music playback off or 30 Hz.

Technique: raise it until the mic sounds thin, then back off one step.

Gate

Depth is the important control on a speech mic, not threshold. Set depth to only 6–12 dB. A gate that slams fully shut on a talking human sounds broken and chops word tails.

Fast attack (a few ms), hold 100–250 ms, release 200–400 ms. In a room with 6+ open mics, use the AMM instead — gates on speech are a blunt tool.

PEQ — speech starting points

4 bands, 20 Hz–20 kHz, ±15 dB. Bands 1 and 4 can be shelving.

Cut 200–350 Hz, 3–6 dB wide — kills drywall boxiness. Almost always an improvement in a breakout room.
Cut 400–800 Hz, 2–4 dB narrow — honky/nasal.
Boost 2–4 kHz, 2–4 dB — intelligibility. Careful, feedback lives here too.
Cut 5–8 kHz, 2–4 dB narrow — harsh sibilance.
Shelf cut above 10 kHz, 2 dB — HVAC hiss and wireless hash.

Finding a problem frequency: take one band, set it narrow (1/9 octave), boost hard (+12 dB), and sweep. The ugly spot jumps out. Then invert to a cut and widen slightly. Cut to fix, boost to flatter — and cutting keeps your headroom.

Compressor — four types

RMS Manual — reacts to average energy, closer to how ears hear. Your meeting-room default.
SlowOpto — gentle, forgiving, very transparent. Presenter mics you want untouched.
Peak Manual — tight and surgical. Transients, limiting, drums.
PunchBag — aggressive and coloured. Never on a corporate panel.

Speech starting point: RMS or SlowOpto, ratio 2.5:1–3:1, soft knee, attack 10–20 ms, release 200–400 ms, threshold set for 3–6 dB gain reduction on loud words and none on quiet ones. Pumping means lengthen the release.

GEQ — output channels only

28 bands, constant 1/3 octave, 31 Hz–16 kHz, ±12 dB. Select the mix, press GEQ Fader Flip, and all 28 bands land on the physical faders with the RTA above them. This is how you ring out a room, and it's dramatically faster than the touchscreen.

Press it again to get your channel faders back before you forget.

Libraries — stop building from scratch

Press Fn on any Processing block to open its Library. A Channel Library recalls the entire strip — gate, PEQ, comp, delay — in one shot. Turn on Recall Preamp if you want gain, pad, 48V and polarity to come with it.

Build a "SPEECH" channel library once and your room setup drops from twenty minutes to two. Note: libraries do not include channel linking or ducker settings.

Copy / Paste / Reset — learn this or waste hours

All three work the same way: hold the key down, then press or touch a target. The target decides how much gets copied.

Hold + targetScope
+ front panel Sel keyAll processing for that channel — but not the preamp
+ SuperStrip In keyJust that one block (e.g. just the PEQ)
+ SuperStrip USB keyThe preamp (gain, pad, 48V, polarity)

Worked example: copy CH2's PEQ to CH3, CH4 and CH5 — hold Copy, touch the PEQ block on CH2's Processing screen. Then hold Paste and press Sel on CH3, CH4, CH5 all while still holding Paste. Four channels EQ'd in about six seconds.

Copy/Paste/Reset never touch channel linking or ducker settings. Those you set by hand.

Mixes and Routing

THE CLASSIC QU MISTAKE — commit this to memory:
Press a blue Mix key and the faders stop being channel levels. They become sends to that mix. When you're done, press the Mix key off or select LR. Forgetting this and then "fixing the mix" on the faders is why the room went quiet.

Assign and pre/post fade

  1. Press the blue Mix key. The master strip becomes that mix's master.
  2. Hold Assign, press the Sel keys you want in this mix. Master strip Sel toggles all at once — useful for starting from all-off.
  3. Hold PreFade and press Sel keys. Key lit = pre-fade.
  4. Set the send tap point on the mix master Routing screen. Post-EQ (default) means the channel compressor doesn't affect the send.
  5. Ride the sends on the faders — then get back to LR.

Pre-fade when it's a monitor or video-call send you want independent of the room mix. Post-fade when it's an FX send or an overflow feed that should follow the room.

DCA vs. Mute Group

DCA (4) — remote-controls the faders of assigned channels, and has its own mute. Carries no audio: no EQ, no compressor, no output socket.

Mute Group (4) — mutes all assigned channels at once. Best use of your four SoftKeys.

Both live at Routing > Fn > Mute/DCA. In a breakout room: one DCA for all speech mics, one for program. Now you run the room with two faders.

The AMM — the feature that makes a meeting room work

Six open mics is roughly 8 dB worse for feedback and room noise than one mic. The Automatic Mic Mixer claws that back by gain-sharing across CH1–16: whoever is talking gets the gain, everyone else gets pulled down, and total open-mic gain stays roughly constant.

  1. Mix master fader DOWN. Non-negotiable — the AMM moves faders when you enable it.
  2. Gain and gain-match every mic first on PAFL. Consistency between mics matters more than absolute level — the algorithm is comparing them.
  3. Setup > Audio > AMM, turn on the speech channels. Faders jump to the "0" open position. Expect it, don't fight it.
  4. Speech mics ONLY. In: lectern, handhelds, lavs, boundary. Out: program audio, laptop, walk-in music, video-call return, anything musical. Put program in the AMM and it will duck your presenters every time a video plays.
  5. Bring the master up slowly. Listen for the ring. Stop below it.
  6. Use a DCA as the master fader for the mics.
DO NOT ride individual AMM faders during the meeting. You're fighting the algorithm and you will lose. Ride the DCA. If one person is genuinely too quiet, fix it with gain or Trim — not the fader.

Ducker

Inserted into input channels; any Qu signal can be the trigger, taken from that channel's Direct Out. Classic breakout use: the lectern mic ducks the walk-in music. Presenter starts talking, music drops 15 dB by itself, no operator required.

Ducker settings are not saved in Channel Libraries and are not copied by Copy/Paste. Set them by hand and write them down.

Scenes, Safes and Shows

The distinction that saves you

Scene Safe — protects all parameters of one channel or mix from any scene recall. Use on walk-in music and the presenter's own mic.

Global Filter — blocks selected parameter groups across all channels for every recall. Classic use: block LR mix processing including GEQ, so your room tuning survives scene changes as the audience fills the room.

Show file — everything: current settings, all 100 scenes, all libraries, all setup. Only ever stored on a USB drive, never inside the mixer. If you didn't make one, you don't have one.

CRITICAL: Reset Mix Settings ignores the Global Filter and ignores channel Safes — and it also resets the filter and clears every safe you had set. It is a real reset. Store a Scene first if you want to keep what's on the desk.

The scene workflow that saves your job

  • Scene 1 "RESET BOARD" — your own zero: your patching, HPFs, comp defaults, DCA assignments, Custom Layer. Now "zero" means your zero.
  • Scene 2 "ROOM CHECK" — after you gain and EQ the room. Your recovery point.
  • Scene 3+ — per segment: walk-in, panel, presentation, video call, Q&A, walk-out.
  • Show file to USB at the end of every day. A $8 USB stick is the difference between a five-minute recovery and a ruined event.

The two resets

Reset Mix Settings — Scenes screen, hold 1–2 seconds, confirm. Clears everything storable in a scene. Survives: stored scenes, libraries, system preferences. Use routinely — before a show, after another operator has had the desk.

System Hard Reset — hold touchscreen Reset + Setup together while powering up, at least 5 seconds through boot. Wipes everything: all scenes, all libraries, all permissions, all passwords. Almost never. This is not a troubleshooting step.

Power Order

Mixer ON FIRST, amps and powered speakers ON LAST.
Powering down: amps OFF FIRST, mixer off last.

And always use Home > Shut Down on the screen before the rear switch, so parameter storing and any USB transfer completes cleanly. Get this wrong and you get a thump that costs you a driver and your reputation in the room.

The 90-Second Card

If you read nothing else on this tab, read this.

  1. Mixer ON. Amps and powered speakers ON last.
  2. Recall Scene 1 "RESET BOARD", or hold Reset Mix Settings 2 seconds and confirm.
  3. Confirm every channel Source = Local.
  4. Name every channel you're using. Six characters.
  5. Gain each mic with PAFL: average 0 dB, peaks +6. Set HPF 100 Hz.
  6. Turn on the AMM for speech mics. Master fader DOWN first.
  7. Build DCA 1 = all mics, DCA 2 = all program.
  8. Bring LR up slowly. Ring out with GEQ Fader Flip + RTA. Max 3–5 cuts.
  9. Video call? Build the mix-minus on Mix 1. Do not skip the de-assign step.
  10. Store a Scene. Then Lock Surface.
  11. End of day: Show file to USB → Home > Shut Down → rear switch. Amps OFF first.
THE THREE MISTAKES
1. Leaving a blue Mix key lit and then "fixing the mix" on the faders — you're editing sends, not the room.
2. Forgetting to de-assign the far-end return from the video-call send, which sends the remote participants their own voice back.
3. Powering the amps before the mixer, or hitting the rear switch without Home > Shut Down.

Walk the Room First

Five minutes that saves an hour.

  • Where are the speakers relative to where the mics will be? Any mic in front of a speaker is a feedback machine. Move one of them — EQ will not save you.
  • How many open mics does the client actually need? Every mic you talk them out of is 1–2 dB of gain before feedback, free.
  • Is there a video-conference system? Codec box, room PC, or someone's laptop — this decides your entire video-call build.
  • What's the ceiling like? Hard drywall and glass mean you'll be cutting 200–350 Hz and fighting reflections.
  • Where's the HVAC and is it on? Rooms sound completely different once air handling kicks in and 40 bodies are seated.
  • Where will the presenter actually stand? Not where the client says — where the lectern and screen are.
  • Assistive listening requirement? That's a Mix bus you need to budget for.
  • Firmware version on the Home screen, and does your iPad app match it?

Mix-Minus for a Video Call

Why this exists: if you send the far end a mix containing their own audio, they hear themselves back with the round-trip delay. That's the echo everybody blames on "the room being bad." The Qu-16 has no acoustic echo cancellation — a correct mix-minus IS your echo control.

The concept in one line: send the far end everything in the room EXCEPT the far end. "Minus" means minus themselves.

One assignment is the entire trick. CH13 goes to LR so the room hears the remote people. CH13 gets de-assigned from Mix 1 so the remote people don't hear themselves. Everything else is ordinary routing.

The build

  1. Patch CH13, source = Local, name VCIN, HPF 80 Hz. Gain it on PAFL with the far end talking.
  2. Assign CH13 to LR only so the room hears the remote people. Do not put CH13 in the AMM.
  3. Press the blue Mix 1 key. Name it VCOUT.
  4. Hold PreFade and press the master strip Sel to set all channels pre-fade. Pre-fade means the far end keeps hearing the room even when you pull the LR fader.
  5. Hold Assign, press Sel for CH1–8 (mics) and ST1/ST2/ST3 (program).
  6. *** DE-ASSIGN CH13 FROM MIX 1 *** — still holding Assign, press CH13 Sel until it's OFF. This single step is the entire mix-minus. Verify it twice.
  7. Also de-assign the FX returns from Mix 1. Reverb down the line makes the far end think the connection is degraded.
  8. Set Mix 1 level so it meters average 0 dB — then check the input meter on the codec side. The Qu outputs +4 dBu and many consumer inputs expect much less. Trim at the far device, not by gutting your mix.
  9. GET OFF THE MIX 1 KEY. Press it off or select LR.
  10. Test with a real human: "Can you hear yourself?" If yes, go back to step 6.

Video call — what the far end says vs. what's wrong

Ringing Out the Room

  1. Select LR on the master strip, open the GEQ tab.
  2. PAFL the mix. The RTA follows the PAFL source — without this you're analysing the wrong signal.
  3. Press GEQ Fader Flip. All 28 bands land on the physical faders.
  4. Raise the mic level until you just start to hear a ring. Don't push into full feedback — you're finding the frequency, not damaging anything.
  5. Read the red dot on the RTA and cut that band a few dB.
  6. STOP AT 3–5 CUTS. Past that you're destroying the tone of the whole system. If you need more than five, the problem is physical.
  7. Press GEQ Fader Flip again to get your channel faders back.
The cheapest dB you will ever find: fewer open mics, mics closer to mouths, and speakers in front of mics rather than behind them. Those three are worth more than any EQ you will ever do. Ringing out is the last 10%, not the first.
