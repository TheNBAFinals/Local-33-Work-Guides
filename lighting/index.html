Fixture Families

Every fixture on a truck is doing one of four jobs: a hard-edged beam you can shape, a soft wash of colour, a narrow shaft you can see in the air, or raw output at the audience. Learn the four jobs and the model names stop mattering.

Beam angle is the whole personality of a fixture. Narrow = you see the shaft in the air and it punches a long throw. Wide = you see the light on the surface and nothing in between. Everything else is refinement.

Conventionals — no motors, no electronics

Ellipsoidal / Leko / Profile hard edge

The workhorse of theatre and corporate. A lens tube with four shutters, a gobo slot, and an iris option. Cuts a crisp shape you can shape off a podium, a banner, or a doorway.

The number on the side is the beam angle — a 19° is a tight long throw, a 36° is a medium, a 50° covers wide and close. Source Four is the model name you'll hear most; the barrel is interchangeable so the same body becomes any angle.

Focus: run the lens tube in/out for edge (sharp vs. soft), rotate the barrel to line the shutters up with what you're cutting.

PAR can

A sealed lamp in a tube. No shutters, no focus — the lamp itself determines the beam. Oval-shaped output you aim by rotating the lamp in the can. Cheap, rugged, and it's what colour-washes rock stages for fifty years running. PAR64 is the standard size; LED PARs have largely replaced them for corporate.

Fresnel

Stepped lens, soft-edged output, and a knob that slides the lamp to go spot to flood. No shutters — you shape it with barn doors. The soft edge means adjacent fixtures blend invisibly, which is why they light film and TV sets.

Cyc lights / striplights / borderlights

Long asymmetric fixtures that wash a cyclorama or backdrop evenly from top to bottom. Asymmetric reflector throws more light at the far part of the cloth so the wash doesn't hot-spot at the top. Usually in 3 or 4 colour circuits.

LED fixtures

LED wash / zoom the corporate default

An array of multi-colour emitters with a motorised zoom. The Artfox Solar and Beam Wash families are typical: 19 × 40 W RGBW, zoom roughly 4° to 60°. One fixture covers tight beam through wide wash.

Colour mixing: RGBW adds white for pastels and better skin tone. RGBWA adds amber. Reading a spec, more emitter colours means better colour rendering and a higher price.

Pixel / ring control means each LED — or each concentric ring — addresses individually, so the fixture becomes an effects device, not just a light. That's what "Ring" in a model name usually signals.

LED PAR / static wash

No pan/tilt, no zoom or a manual one. Uplighting a ballroom, washing drape, colouring a set piece. Cheap enough to deploy in dozens, and battery-powered versions exist for anywhere you can't run cable.

Blinder / strobe at the audience

Raw output pointed at the crowd. Traditional blinders are 2, 4 or 8 PAR-64 lamps in a cluster; modern ones are COB LED panels with individual cell control and a strobe engine.

Handle with judgment. A blinder hit is an effect, not a look. And warn anyone with photosensitivity concerns — strobe is a genuine medical trigger, and most venues require signage.

Reading a moving light spec sheet

Light source — discharge lamp (the "R" numbers: 14R, 18R, 20R = lamp wattage class) or LED. Discharge is brighter per dollar and hotter; LED is dimmable to zero, instant-on, and colour-stable.

CMY — cyan/magenta/yellow flags that slide in to subtract colour. Continuous mixing, any shade. Colour wheel — fixed dichroic filters that snap to a colour. Most fixtures have both.

CTO — colour temperature orange. Warms a cold source down toward tungsten so it matches conventionals.

Gobo wheels — rotating gobos can spin and index; static ones just snap in. "9 rotating + 1 open" means nine patterns plus a clear position.

Prism — splits the beam into multiple images. 3-facet, 8-facet, linear, circular. Rotates.

Frost — diffusion that softens a hard edge toward a wash.

Animation wheel — a moving texture plate for fire, water, cloud effects.

IP rating — IP65 means it lives outside in the rain. IP20 means it does not.

The one thing to check before you hang anything: the DMX channel count and mode. The same fixture might be 16 channels in basic mode and 45 in extended. Hang forty of them in the wrong mode and your patch, your addressing, and your universe count are all wrong. Confirm the mode with the LD before you address the first fixture.

Followspots

Running a spot

The controls: iris (beam size), douser (fade in/out without changing the iris), colour boomerang (flip-in colour frames), and the focus knob for edge.

Pick-up: you're told a size (head, head-and-shoulders, waist, full body) and a colour frame. Set the iris before you open the douser — never hunt for the size with the light live on the artist.

Framing the body: keep the person centred with a little more room above the head than below the feet. Lead their movement slightly rather than chasing it.

Comms is the job. You're on headset taking calls from the spot caller. Acknowledge every call, and if you lose your pickup, say so immediately — silence is worse than a mistake.

Remote followspot systems (Follow-Me and similar) put a camera and a tracker on the rig and let one operator drive moving lights as spots from front of house. 4Wall carries both the fader and mouse consoles for it.

Effects

  • Blacklight / UV — makes white and fluorescent materials glow. Popular for themed events and glow-paint activations.
  • Lasers — a separate discipline with real regulation. In the US, laser shows over audiences require a variance, and audience-scanning has strict power limits. Never operate one you haven't been trained on.
  • Strobes — the medical caution applies. Signage and a heads-up to the client.
  • Mirror balls, gobo rotators, effect wheels — mechanical texture, still very much in use.

DMX — how fixtures actually get told what to do

DMX512 is a one-way conversation. The console talks, every fixture listens, and nothing ever talks back. Understand that sentence and most DMX problems explain themselves.

The whole protocol in four facts:
1. A universe is 512 channels. That's the hard ceiling.
2. Each fixture claims a block of consecutive channels starting at its address.
3. Data travels in one direction down a daisy chain — out of the console, into fixture 1, out of fixture 1, into fixture 2, and so on.
4. The last fixture in the chain needs a terminator, or the signal reflects back up the line and corrupts everything.

Addressing — the part people get wrong

The maths you actually do on a floor: next address = this address + this fixture's channel count. A 16-channel fixture at 1 puts the next one at 17. Get the channel count wrong and every fixture after it is wrong too.

How many fixtures fit in a universe?

512 ÷ channel count. A 16-channel wash → 32 fixtures. A 45-channel extended-mode moving light → 11 fixtures. That's why mode matters so much: switching modes can double your universe count and your node requirement.

Fixtures can share an address deliberately. Ten uplights all at 001 will do exactly the same thing forever, which is often what you want — one channel block controls the whole colour wash. Just know you're doing it on purpose.

DMX Cable

5-pin vs. 3-pin

5-pin XLR is the DMX standard. Pins 1/2/3 carry the data; 4/5 are a spare pair that almost nothing uses. Professional fixtures and consoles use 5-pin.

3-pin DMX exists on cheaper fixtures and looks exactly like an audio XLR. It is not one — and this is the mistake that matters.

Never run DMX on mic cable. They fit. They will even appear to work on a short run with a couple of fixtures. But mic cable is ~60 Ω and DMX needs 110–120 Ω impedance. The mismatch causes reflections, and you get the classic symptom: fixtures flickering, dropping out, or twitching randomly — usually the ones furthest down the chain, usually only once the room is full and you're in show.

It's the hardest fault to find because it's intermittent and the cable "works." Use real DMX cable, and keep your DMX and audio XLR visibly different — different colour, different tape, different case.

The terminator

What it is and why it matters

A 120 Ω resistor in a male XLR shell, plugged into the DMX OUT of the last fixture in the chain. It absorbs the signal at the end of the line so it doesn't bounce back up the cable and collide with incoming data.

Symptom of a missing terminator: intermittent flicker and random behaviour that gets worse with longer runs and more fixtures. Short chains often work fine without one — which is exactly why people stop bothering, right up until the show where it doesn't.

Terminate every chain, every time. It costs nothing and removes an entire category of fault from your night.

Daisy chain rules

  • 32 devices maximum per chain. Past that the signal degrades — use a splitter/opto-isolator to start a fresh branch.
  • ~300 m / 1000 ft total run is the practical limit for a chain.
  • Out to In, always. DMX OUT of the console → DMX IN of fixture 1. OUT of fixture 1 → IN of fixture 2. There is no such thing as a DMX "loop back to the console."
  • No Y-splits. Never split DMX passively with a Y-cable. Use a powered opto-splitter — it isolates each branch electrically, so one bad fixture or shorted cable can't take out the whole rig.
  • One universe per chain. A chain carries one universe. Multiple universes need multiple runs, or a node with multiple ports.

Opto-splitters — the piece that saves shows

A splitter takes one DMX input and gives you 4, 8 or more electrically isolated outputs. Two reasons to use one:

1. Topology. A rig laid out as a star from a splitter is far easier to troubleshoot than one long snake — you can lose one branch instead of everything.

2. Isolation. "Opto" means the input and outputs are optically coupled with no electrical connection. A fixture that fails and shorts its data line kills its own branch and nothing else. On a long daisy chain, that same fault kills every fixture downstream of it.

Networked Lighting Control

Art-Net and sACN

Once you're past a few universes, running physical DMX from the console stops making sense. Instead the console sends many universes over ethernet to nodes out in the rig, and each node converts back to physical DMX at the truss.

Art-Net and sACN (E1.31) are the two protocols. sACN is the more modern one and handles large systems better. Both ride on standard Cat5e/Cat6.

Nodes — ETC Net3 gateways, ProPlex IQ, MA NPUs. Each has some number of physical DMX ports, and you assign a universe to each port. That assignment is the thing people forget: a node with the wrong universe on a port looks exactly like a dead cable.

Keep the lighting network separate. Its own switch, its own subnet, no internet, no video traffic. Lighting data is time-critical and shares badly.

Wireless DMX

A transmitter at the console end and receivers at fixtures you can't cable to. Common on battery uplights, set pieces, moving props, and anywhere a cable would be seen or tripped over.

What to know:

• It runs in the 2.4 GHz band — the same space as WiFi, comms, and everyone's phone. A full room degrades it, exactly like wireless mics.
• Transmitter and receivers must be on the same universe and the same RF channel/ID, and you generally pair them before the show.
• It carries the universe wirelessly; fixture addressing is unchanged — you still address each fixture normally.
• Line of sight matters. Get the transmitter up and out, not behind a road case.
• Always cable anything you can cable. Wireless DMX is for the fixtures where cable is genuinely impossible, not a shortcut to save a cable run.

The habit that separates working techs from the rest: DMX and power never share a jacket, and ideally not a cable path. Run them on opposite sides of the truss. Mains next to data induces noise into the data, and the symptom — intermittent flicker — looks exactly like every other DMX fault. Rule it out by construction instead of chasing it at 11pm.

Home Runs

The runs from the source to where they land. Power leaves the distro, data leaves the console, and both have to arrive at the right position on the right cable. Then you daisy-chain locally — and how many fixtures fit on one circuit comes from the fixture's manual, not from what looks reasonable.

What a home run actually is. The cable from the source to its destination, run once, in one piece. Power home runs leave the distro — usually as Socapex carrying six circuits — and land at a breakout on the position. Data home runs leave the console at FOH, land at a splitter near the stage, and branch out from there. Everything after the breakout or the splitter is a local run, and that is where the daisy chains live.

Why the splitter goes near the stage, not at FOH

One long DMX home run from the console to a splitter at the deck, then short branches to each position. That is one long cable instead of four.

The real payoff is troubleshooting. An opto-splitter isolates each branch, so a fault on one position cannot take down the others. When a truss goes dark you know instantly which branch it is on, and the rest of the rig keeps working while you find it.

Why the manual beats your arithmetic

You can work out that 16 A ÷ 3.75 A means four movers fit on a 20 A circuit. Then the manual says three.

The manual is lower because it accounts for inrush — the surge when several fixtures strike at once — and for the current rating of the link-through connector on the fixture itself, which is often the real limit rather than the breaker. The connector melts before the breaker trips.

Your maths tells you the ceiling. The manual tells you the answer. When they disagree, the manual wins.

powerCON vs True1 in a daisy chain

Both chain fixture to fixture. The difference is what you are allowed to do while the circuit is live.

Original powerCON is not rated to connect or disconnect under load. If you need to break that chain, kill the circuit first.

True1 is rated for it, and sealed. This is why it became the standard on modern fixtures — you can pull one out of a live chain without shutting down the position.

Blue is in, grey is out on powerCON, and they are keyed so you cannot chain backwards.

Power — Distro and Dimmers

Scope, before anything else. Tying in to building service, landing cam-lok, and anything on the line side of a distro is licensed electrician work. As a hand you plug into distro that somebody qualified has already energised, you run cable, and you load circuits correctly. Know the whole picture so you're useful and safe — but know where your job stops.
Everything left of the distro output is somebody else's ticket. Everything right of it is the job you'll actually be doing — and doing it correctly is what keeps the left side from becoming a problem.

Distro

What a distro does

Takes one large service feed and breaks it into many protected circuits. A road-case distro will typically give you a mix of Socapex outputs (6 circuits each) and individual Edison or stage-pin outlets, each on its own breaker, usually with metering per leg.

Read the meters. Three-phase service means three legs, and you want the load roughly even across them. Badly unbalanced legs are how you trip a main with plenty of apparent capacity left.

Load maths you should be able to do in your head

Watts ÷ Volts = Amps. At 120 V:

• A 1000 W fixture ≈ 8.3 A
• A 575 W Source Four ≈ 4.8 A
• A 300 W LED wash ≈ 2.5 A

Derate to 80%. A 20 A circuit is good for a continuous 16 A. A 15 A circuit is good for 12 A. Lighting runs for hours, so it counts as continuous.

So: a 20 A circuit holds three 575 W lekos comfortably (14.4 A), not four (19.2 A — over the derate and one bump from tripping).

LED changed the arithmetic. A truss of LED washes might pull less than one circuit of old PAR cans. But LED power supplies have inrush current at switch-on that can trip a breaker even when the running load is tiny — which is why you stagger power-up on a big LED rig instead of throwing one switch.

Dimmers

What dimming is and when it applies

A dimmer rack takes constant power and varies the voltage to a conventional fixture, controlled by DMX. One DMX channel per dimmer circuit — dimmer 1 is channel 1, dimmer 24 is channel 24. Simple and unchanged for decades.

The critical distinction: never plug an LED fixture, a moving light, or anything with a power supply into a dimmed circuit. They need constant power and they control their own intensity over DMX. A dimmed circuit will damage them or make them behave erratically.

That's why distros and dimmer racks have switched (constant) circuits as well as dimmed ones — sometimes called "hot" or "non-dim." Intelligent fixtures go there. Confirm which is which before you plug anything in.

The corporate reality: most breakout and ballroom work now is all LED and all non-dim. You'll see dimmer racks on theatre, TV and older house rigs. Knowing the distinction still matters, because the day you plug a $6,000 moving light into a dimmer is the day you find out.

Cable practice that keeps you employed

  • Label both ends of every run. Same rule as audio, same reason.
  • Power and data on opposite sides of the truss. Induced noise into DMX is an invisible fault.
  • Service loops at every fixture. Enough slack that a focus or a trim change doesn't strain a connector.
  • Safety cable on everything that flies. No exceptions, no "it's only up for an hour."
  • Tie cable off to the truss — not hanging on the connector. The connector is not a load-bearing device.
  • Dress runs so a road case can roll past. If a cable has to cross a walk path, it goes under matting or up and over.
  • Coil over-under. Stranded copper survives coiling; it does not survive kinks.

Haze and Fog

Beams don't exist until there's something in the air to catch them. Atmospherics aren't decoration — for a beam-heavy rig they're the difference between a light show and a bunch of expensive fixtures pointing at nothing.

Hazer

Produces a fine, even, long-hanging suspension that fills a room invisibly and reveals every beam. You should not be able to see "haze" — you should just see the light.

Run it early and low. Build a base level well before doors and top it up gently. Chasing haze after the room fills never looks right and always looks obvious.

Fogger

Produces visible clouds that dissipate. This is an effect you see — a blast for a reveal, low fog for a floor look. Different job entirely from haze.

Low-lying fog needs the fluid chilled (dry ice, CO₂, or a chiller unit) or it just rises and becomes room fog.

The fire alarm conversation happens before the fluid goes in. Haze and fog set off smoke detection. In any hotel, convention centre or venue this means:

• Notify the venue and get approval in writing.
• Detection in the affected zone may need to be isolated by the venue's fire safety officer — never by you, never by the LD.
• Some venues require a fire watch posted while detection is bypassed.
• Understand it may simply be refused, and the design has to survive that answer.

An accidental evacuation of a 2,000-person conference is a career event. Ask early, get it in writing, and never assume the last crew's arrangement carries over.
  • Water-based fluid is standard for most venues; oil-based hangs longer but is banned in many rooms and leaves residue. Check what the venue allows before you load fluid.
  • HVAC is your enemy. Air handling strips haze fast. Ask whether it can be reduced during the show — often it can.
  • Point the output away from people and away from intakes. Nobody wants a face full of it, and an intake distributes it everywhere except where you wanted it.
  • Fluid is consumable and specific. Wrong fluid clogs the machine. Bring the right one and bring spare.
  • Hazers need warm-up. Power them early — they're not instant.

Lighting Call — Set Order

Same principle as every other department: place what you can't easily move, then work backward.

  1. Read the plot. Fixture types, positions, quantities, universe assignments, addresses. If there's no plot, get the LD's intent before you touch a case.
  2. Truss on the deck. Hang fixtures while the truss is at working height — never reach up to a flown truss to hang.
  3. Hang and safety. Clamp to the truss, safety cable through the fixture yoke and around the truss chord, every fixture, no exceptions.
  4. Address at the deck. Set every fixture's address and mode while you can reach the display. Doing this in the air costs ten times as long.
  5. Cable the truss. Power one side, DMX the other. Service loops. Tie it off. Terminate the end of each chain.
  6. Power up and prove it before the truss leaves the ground. Every fixture strikes, every fixture responds to its address. A fixture that doesn't work on the deck is free to fix; at trim it's a lift call.
  7. Trim. Fly to height, level it, mark the trim.
  8. Focus. Fixture by fixture with the LD. Conventionals get shuttered and sharpened; movers get positions recorded into palettes.
  9. Patch and cue. LD's world — you're supporting: running a lamp, holding a focus, updating a palette.
  10. Note everything on the plot. What changed, what's dead, what got re-addressed. The next call is you.
Step 6 is the one that separates crews. Proving the rig at deck height costs twenty minutes. Discovering a dead fixture at trim costs a lift, a stopped rehearsal, and a conversation with the production manager. Test everything before it flies.

Positions

LD — Lighting Designer

Owns the look. Draws the plot, chooses fixtures and positions, sits at the console, builds and calls the cues.

L1 — Lead / Master Electrician

Owns execution of the plot. Runs the crew, makes the practical calls on hang and power, and is the LD's counterpart on the floor.

L2 — Lighting System Tech / Board Op

Sets up and tests the control system, patches, and often runs the desk under the LD's direction. On corporate, frequently the person actually pressing GO.

Lighting Tech / Electrician

Hangs, cables, addresses, focuses, strikes. Where most calls start, and where you learn whether someone can be trusted with the next step up.

Followspot Operator

On a spot, on headset, taking calls. Discussed in the Fixtures tab.

Consoles

Every lighting console does the same five things in the same order. Learn that once and any desk becomes navigable — you're just hunting for where this manufacturer put each step.

Patch is the foundation. If the console thinks fixture 12 is a 16-channel wash and it's actually 24-channel, every parameter after channel 16 lands on the wrong thing — and the symptom looks like a broken fixture, not a patch error.

Live vs. Blind — the concept that confuses everyone once

Live is the real output. What you change, the room sees.

Blind is editing a cue without affecting output. You can rebuild cue 40 while cue 12 is on stage and nobody in the room knows.

The trap: making changes in Blind and wondering why the stage did not react — or worse, making changes in Live during a show thinking you were in Blind. Check which mode you are in before you touch anything.

Tracking

Eos is a tracking console. A value stays as it is until something explicitly changes it. Record cue 1 with channel 5 at full and it is still at full in cue 8 unless you changed it.

Why it matters: cue lists stay compact, and an edit ripples forward into every later cue that inherited the value. Powerful, and occasionally surprising.

When the LD says "that tracked forward" they mean an edit propagated into later cues. That is the desk working correctly, not a fault.

Record Only and Cue Only / Track are the tools that let you make a change in one cue without it tracking onward.

Palettes — where the time savings live

Four kinds, matching the four parameter families: Intensity, Focus, Colour, Beam (IFCB). A palette stores values by reference.

A focus palette called PODIUM holds a pan/tilt for every fixture. Cues reference the palette rather than raw numbers. The podium moves six feet at rehearsal — you update one palette and every cue that references it follows.

This is very often the assistant's job: updating focus palettes after a set change while the LD keeps working.

Eos hardware you'll meet

Eos Apex — current flagship. Eos Ti, Gio @5, Ion Xe — the working desks you'll see on corporate and theatre calls. Element 2 — simplified, education and small houses. ETCnomad — the software on a laptop with a USB dongle; same syntax, no hardware faders unless you add a wing.

The important part: the syntax is identical across all of them. Learn it once on a free copy of ETCnomad at home and you can walk up to an Apex.

Practice at home for free. ETC publishes ETCnomad — the actual Eos software — and it runs in a demo/offline mode without a dongle. You can patch a rig, build cues and practise the entire syntax on your own laptop. That is the single highest-value thing you can do between calls. ETC also publishes free video tutorials and practice projects built on real production scenarios.

Their paid Level 1–4 courses are instructor-led with their own workbooks. Worth doing if an employer will fund it — but the syntax above and hands-on Nomad time will get you working long before that.

ETC ColorSource

ColorSource is the opposite design philosophy from Eos. Eos gives you a command line and expects you to build sentences. ColorSource gives you faders, bump buttons and a touchscreen and expects you to touch things directly. It shares the ETC badge and the fixture library — it does not share the syntax. Do not walk up expecting Eos commands.

The two models

ColorSource 20 and ColorSource 40 — the number is the fader count. Same software, same layout, different width. The 40 gives you more looks under your fingers without paging.

Power up — the order that matters

  1. DMX cable into the DMX port first.
  2. Power supply into the DC input on the rear.
  3. Plug the supply into your source.
  4. It powers itself on as soon as it sees power — there is no power switch to hunt for. Surprises people the first time.
  5. From the startup screen, press Start to get into the console software.

Power down — do not just pull the plug

Press and hold the Stage Map / Shutdown button. When the shutdown display opens, choose shutdown or cancel.

Shutdown puts the console into sleep mode. Only then is it safe to remove power. If you leave it asleep, pressing that same button wakes it.

Why it matters: yanking power on any console mid-write risks the show file. Thirty seconds of shutdown protects a day of programming.

The vocabulary — this is where ColorSource differs most

These words mean specific things on this desk, and several of them do not mean what they mean on Eos. Learning this table is most of learning the console.

TermWhat it means here
ChannelOne numeric handle the console controls. Could be a dimmer, a group of dimmers, or a whole moving light.
DimmerSingle-address, intensity only. A conventional on a dimmer circuit.
DeviceMulti-address with parameters — position, colour, beam, intensity. An LED wash or a mover.
BumpThe buttons directly under the faders.
PlaybackOne look — or a sequence of several looks — living on a fader.
SequenceA playback made of steps, with fade and step timing. This is how you get a chase.
CueA recorded stage look: intensity, colour, beam and effects together.
CrossfaderManual control over the fade between cues instead of using Go.
GoAdvances the cue list using the recorded times.
IndependentsControl for on/off devices — motors, smoke machines, foggers.
EffectsPatterns applied to selected lights. Four kinds: colour, shape (motion), intensity, parameter.
Dimmer vs. Device is the distinction to internalise. A Dimmer is one address and intensity only. A Device claims several addresses and has parameters you can push. Patch a mover as a dimmer and you get intensity and nothing else — no colour, no position, and it will look like the fixture is broken.

It is the same trap as fixture mode on the rig, showing up in the patch instead.

Bump button modes — Flash vs. Solo

The bump buttons have a mode setting, and the difference is dramatic in a live room:

Flash — pressing a bump brings that playback's channels up to the level of the Bump Master. Everything else keeps doing what it was doing.

Solo — pressing a bump brings that playback up and takes every other playback to zero.

Know which mode you are in before you touch a bump during a show. Reaching for a flash and getting a solo blacks out everything else on stage in front of the room.

Independents — the one people forget exists

Independents drive things that are on or off rather than dimmed: fog and smoke machines, motors, practical effects.

This connects directly to your atmospherics: a fogger on an independent can be triggered from the desk without living on a fader where somebody might drag it up by accident.

What to expect on a call

ColorSource turns up where the room needs competent, simple control rather than deep programming — corporate breakouts, worship, small venues, and anywhere the client drives it after you leave.

The handover is the job. Record clean looks onto faders, label the strip, set the bump mode deliberately, and leave a card saying what each fader does and who to call.

Verify against the desk in front of you. ETC revises the ColorSource line and exact I/O, fader counts and features differ by model and software version. The operating model and vocabulary above are stable; specific numbers are not. Check the console's own About screen and match it to the current manual — ETC publishes all of them free.

The five questions for any console you have never touched

1. Where is patch, and what does it think is out there?
2. How do I select a fixture or a group?
3. How do I get a parameter onto an encoder or a fader?
4. How do I record what I am looking at?
5. How do I play it back, and how do I get out of trouble — undo, clear, release?

Answer those five and you are useful on any desk in the world inside ten minutes. Everything else is depth you can add while the LD talks.
